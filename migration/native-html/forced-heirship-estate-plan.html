How Forced Heirship Affects Your Estate Plan

Understanding Puerto Rico’s legítima and planning strategies that work within it.

← Back to Resources

What Is the Legítima?

The legítima is the portion of your estate that Puerto Rico law reserves for forced heirs (herederos forzosos). Under the Civil Code (Ley 55-2020), your estate is divided into two parts:

  • Legítima (1/2): Divided equally among all forced heirs (children and surviving spouse). No discretion.
  • Libre disposición (1/2): Can go to anyone you choose.

Who Are Forced Heirs?

Children and the surviving spouse are forced heirs under the 2020 Civil Code. If a child predeceases you, grandchildren step in by right of representation. Parents become forced heirs only if you have no descendants. The 2020 Code elevated the surviving spouse from usufruct-only rights to full forced heir status, sharing the legítima equally with children.

Common Conflicts for Mainland Transplants

  • “Everything to my spouse” plans — Children are entitled to their legítima at your death, not after the surviving spouse dies.
  • Disinheritance — Only permitted for specific, enumerated grounds (e.g., attempted murder of the parent).
  • Unequal distributions — The legítima must be divided equally among all forced heirs.
  • Trust-based plans — A trust does not override the legítima.

Planning Strategies

  • Use the libre disposición strategically to favor one heir (e.g., a child in the family business or a surviving spouse).
  • Lifetime giving can reduce the estate, but collation rules may apply.
  • Irrevocable trusts under Law 219-2012 can provide income to a spouse while preserving forced heirs’ rights.
  • Dual-will architecture separates Puerto Rico assets from non-PR assets.
  • Life insurance proceeds are generally not part of the legítima calculation.
