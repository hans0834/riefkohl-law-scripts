Prenuptial & Postnuptial Agreements in Puerto Rico: What Mainland Intuition Gets Wrong

Puerto Rico is a community property jurisdiction — and moving here can rewrite your marital property regime without anyone signing anything. What capitulaciones matrimoniales do, what they can’t, and how to execute one that holds up.

By Hans Riefkohl, Riefkohl Law • August 2026

Start here: Puerto Rico is community property by default

If you married in a common-law state, your baseline assumption is probably “what’s titled in my name is mine.” Puerto Rico’s baseline is the opposite. Unless spouses affirmatively choose another regime, the Civil Code of 2020 places them under the sociedad legal de gananciales — a community property regime — automatically, from the moment of the marriage ceremony (Arts. 488-489, 508). You don’t opt in; you land there by not opting out.

Under that regime, both spouses own the community assets equally, and on dissolution — divorce or death — the community is split down the middle (Art. 507). Property owned before the marriage, and property inherited or received by gift, stays separate (privativo, Art. 509). But three rules in Article 513 surprise almost every mainland transplant:

  • Earnings are community property. Salary, professional fees, and the compensation you draw from your own business are community assets — even if the business itself is separate property (Art. 513(b)).
  • The fruits of separate property are community property. Dividends, interest, rent, and distributions thrown off by a separate asset flow into the community (Art. 513(c)). It isn’t the title that builds your spouse’s claim — it’s the cash flow.
  • A business founded during the marriage with community funds is community property (Art. 513(e)) — and mixed funding creates co-ownership (Art. 516).

On top of that, everything owned during the marriage is presumed community until proven separate (Art. 519) — a proof that gets very hard after years of commingling. And if a separate asset grows in value because of community funds or a spouse’s undercompensated work, the community earns a credit against that appreciation (Art. 518). The founder who works for years in a “separate” company while taking a below-market salary is quietly building a community claim on its growth.

The five-year clock: moving here can change your regime silently

For couples who married elsewhere, Puerto Rico’s conflict-of-law rules decide which regime governs. The default is the law of the first conjugal domicile — but if the spouses establish a common domicile in another jurisdiction for five (5) years or more, the marital property regime becomes that of the new jurisdiction, unless they agree otherwise (Art. 43, Civil Code of 2020; third-party rights are protected).

Read that again: relocating to Puerto Rico can convert your marriage to a community property regime by the mere passage of time. For an Act 60 decree holder arriving with appreciated assets, a mainland trust, or an operating business, that clock is usually the single best reason to deal with this early — ideally as part of the same planning engagement as your estate plan review. A U.S. prenup is not automatically effective here either: its content is judged under the law of the conjugal domicile, and agreements executed in Puerto Rico face strict local formalities (more below). Have it reviewed — don’t assume.

Prenups AND postnups: the rule that changed in 2018

Under the old Civil Code of 1930, marital agreements (capitulaciones matrimoniales) were immutable — sign before the wedding or never. That rule is gone. Since Act 62-2018, now Article 491 of the 2020 Code, spouses may adopt, modify, or replace their economic regime at any time, before or during the marriage — no court approval, no waiting period, no required justification.

Two limits matter. First, the agreement doesn’t bind third parties until it’s recorded in the Marital Agreements Registry (Registro de Capitulaciones Matrimoniales). Second, switching to separate property never prejudices creditors’ existing rights (Art. 546) — it protects forward, not backward. An agreement signed with a lawsuit or debt already on the horizon isn’t planning; it’s an additional problem.

And a common misconception: a marital agreement doesn’t have to mean total separation of property. You can keep a community over earnings while making investment income separate, or any other combination. The tool is a dial, not a switch.

Two formalities decide whether it works at all

1. A public deed before a Puerto Rico notary

Capitulaciones and their amendments must be executed as a public deed (escritura pública) to be valid (Art. 499). This is not an evidentiary technicality you can cure later. In López Torres v. González Vázquez, 151 D.P.R. 225 (2000), the Puerto Rico Supreme Court voided a premarital agreement signed as a private document before a Maryland notary public — the notarization only proved the signatures, and without a public deed there was no agreement. If your prenup is a stapled PDF signed at a bank, assume it does not work here until a lawyer tells you otherwise.

2. Registration

The deed binds the spouses between themselves; to have effect against banks, buyers, and creditors it must also be recorded in the Marital Agreements Registry, attached to the Puerto Rico Supreme Court (Arts. 499, 501) — and in the Property Registry if it affects real estate. A third party who contracted believing the marriage was still community-property is protected if the change wasn’t recorded. Recording isn’t a closing formality; it’s half the value of the document.

What this means for your estate plan

When a spouse married under the community regime dies, there are two liquidations, not one: first the community is inventoried, credits between estates are settled, and the community is split in half (Arts. 533-539); only then does the decedent’s half, plus their separate property, become the estate. Your will or trust can only dispose of what actually belongs to you (Art. 530).

Then Puerto Rico’s forced heirship applies. Under the 2020 Code, the surviving spouse is a forced heir with an ownership share, on equal footing with the children (Art. 1624) — so a surviving spouse holds two distinct, cumulative claims: their community half (which is not inheritance) and their forced share (which is). A marital agreement operates on the first claim only. It cannot touch the second: an advance waiver of the forced share is ineffective (Art. 1627), and the forced share cannot be encumbered with conditions (Art. 1626). If you’re used to mainland practice, this is the headline difference — in Puerto Rico, a prenup cannot waive the surviving spouse’s inheritance rights.

What a marital agreement does accomplish is just as valuable: it defines and shrinks the mass the forced-heirship rules operate on, makes clear what enters the estate, and prevents the poorly documented community liquidation that freezes successions for years. It also clears the path for funding: gratuitous transfers of community assets — gifts to children, funding an irrevocable trust — are void without both spouses’ consent (Art. 529). With the regime defined by deed and recorded, your trust is funded with assets whose ownership isn’t in dispute.

Business owners: the shareholder agreement that gets neutralized

A shareholders’ or operating agreement can have airtight transfer restrictions, a right of first refusal, and a buy-sell with a valuation formula — and still be practically defeated by a spouse’s community claim, because that claim doesn’t arise from a transfer. It arises from the law, from the fact of the marriage and the passage of time, and it never walks through the door those clauses guard. That’s why sophisticated agreements increasingly require each married partner to deliver recorded capitulaciones or, at minimum, a spousal consent — the former being substantially stronger.

  • Protect the fruits, not just the equity. Even if your interest is separate property, its distributions during the marriage are generally community (Art. 513(c)) — as is your compensation. A succession plan that only addresses the equity solves half the problem.
  • Inventory the credits. Capital contributions, honored personal guarantees, and improvements paid with community money create credits in favor of the community, collected at liquidation (Arts. 518, 538). List them and allocate them expressly in the deed.
  • One valuation method. Closely held interests are the hard thing to appraise. Fix the method in the marital agreement and use the same one as the buy-sell, so two documents don’t put two prices on the same interest.
  • Ownership thresholds. Certifications and programs that turn on who owns what can be jeopardized by an undeclared community interest. Resolve it before the application, not after the audit. (Choosing your entity? See LLC vs. Corporation in Puerto Rico.)

What a Puerto Rico marital agreement cannot do

  • It cannot defeat creditors’ already-acquired rights (Art. 546).
  • It cannot waive or encumber the forced share (legítima) in advance (Arts. 1626-1627).
  • It cannot eliminate the duty to contribute to family expenses or child support obligations (Art. 492).
  • It cannot impair either spouse’s dignity or equal rights — such clauses are treated as unwritten (Art. 498).
  • It does not replace the separate consents federal law requires for ERISA-qualified retirement plans — survivor annuities and beneficiary designations follow federal rules and plan documents.
  • It is not, by itself, a tax plan — income attribution, incentive decrees, and trust consequences need separate analysis with your CPA.

Making it challenge-proof

The validity of capitulaciones is governed, supplementarily, by general contract law (Art. 502) — which is the door every future challenge walks through: mistake, deceit, duress, “I didn’t know what there was.” Four practices close it:

  • Mutual financial disclosure. Not a validity requirement in Puerto Rico — but a complete reciprocal inventory, attached as an exhibit to the deed, is the single best defense against a consent-based attack. Cheap prophylaxis compared to the litigation it prevents.
  • Independent counsel for each spouse. No statute or Supreme Court opinion requires it for validity — anyone telling you a Puerto Rico prenup is automatically void without separate lawyers is applying stateside law that doesn’t govern here. But a record showing each side had its own counsel, full disclosure, and an unhurried calendar is what closes the Art. 502 door. When one office drafts from one spouse’s side, the other spouse should be notified in writing of the right to independent counsel, with their decision documented.
  • Understand the notary’s role. Under Puerto Rico’s civil-law notarial system (Act 75-1987), the notary is nobody’s advocate — they represent the public faith, impartially, for all parties. Their legal warnings are mandatory and valuable, but they are not a substitute for a lawyer who answers to you alone.
  • Time, with a record proving it. Enough demonstrable time between first draft and signing to consult, question, and negotiate. Eve-of-wedding agreements are the most vulnerable.

The practical sequence

Step 1: Diagnose the current regime
Certification from the Marital Agreements Registry (Form OAT-1842; $5 in revenue stamps), confirmation of when and where you married and where you were domiciled, and a choice-of-law determination — including where the five-year clock stands. Nobody assumes there’s no agreement: it gets certified.
Step 2: Inventory and mutual disclosure
Assets, debts, income, and business interests of both spouses, documented, to be attached as an exhibit to the deed.
Step 3: Documented independent counsel
Written notice of the right to independent counsel to the unrepresented spouse, with the draft delivered reasonably in advance.
Step 4: Negotiate and draft
The replacement regime; liquidation of the existing community; credits between estates; express treatment of business interests and their distributions; an express reservation of ERISA-governed plans.
Step 5: Execute before a notary
Public deed, with notarial tariff and stamps under the Notarial Act based on the value stated in the instrument.
Step 6: Record
Filing with the Marital Agreements Registry and, if real estate is affected, the Property Registry. Without this step the document doesn’t work against third parties.
Step 7: Harmonize
Cross-check against the shareholders’ agreement, buy-sell, entity documents, will, trust, and beneficiary designations — so every document says the same thing.
